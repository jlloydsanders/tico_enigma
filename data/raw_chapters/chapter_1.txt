Chapter 1: The Blood in the Soil
The rain in the Central Valley doesn’t just fall; it assaults the earth. Inspector Teo Rojas sat in the driver’s seat of his rusted Toyota Land Cruiser, the wipers fighting a losing battle against the October downpour. He rubbed his temples, trying to massage away a headache born of cheap guaro liquor and three hours of sleep. At fifty-two, Rojas had spent thirty years with the Organismo de Investigación Judicial (OIJ)—long enough to know that a phone call at three in the morning never brought good news.

He killed the engine and stepped out into the ankle-deep mud of Finca El Paraíso, a sprawling, high-altitude coffee plantation situated on the misty slopes of the Poás Volcano. Yellow police tape was already strung between the glossy green coffee shrubs, flapping wildly in the wind.

Rojas ducked under the tape, his trench coat heavy with water. A young uniformed officer holding a flashlight pale and trembling, looked relieved to see him.

"Inspector," the kid said, his voice cracking. "It’s bad. Captain Salazar said to wait for you before we move him."

"Salazar only waits for me so he has someone to blame when the paperwork gets lost," Rojas grumbled, flicking a soggy cigarette into the brush. "Show me."

They walked down a narrow, terraced path. The smell of wet volcanic soil and unroasted coffee cherries was thick, but underneath it lay the sharp, metallic tang of copper.

The victim was lying face down in the mud. He was wearing a bespoke charcoal suit that cost more than Rojas made in a year. Rojas knelt, the cold mud seeping through his trousers, and gently turned the man’s head with a gloved hand.

It was Elias Delgado.

Rojas let out a low whistle. Delgado was the financial architect for the Los Lobos syndicate, a ruthless, organized crime family that had recently moved their laundering operations down from Mexico to establish a foothold in San José.

"Execution style," Captain Salazar announced, materializing from the gloom with a smug look on his face. He pointed a pen at the back of Delgado’s head. "Two rounds from a nine-millimeter, close range. A classic cartel message. The Culebras gang has been trying to take over the western port routes. They finally made their move."

"A hit," Rojas muttered, his eyes scanning the body.

"Textbook," Salazar said, already closing his notebook. "Write it up as a syndicate turf war. The brass will be thrilled. It gives them the excuse they need to raid the Culebras safehouses in Puntarenas. Case closed, Teo. Go home and sleep off whatever it is you smell like."

Rojas didn't move. He kept his eyes locked on Delgado. Something was wrong. Yes, the two bullets to the back of the head screamed 'mafia hit.' It was the perfect narrative. Too perfect.

"If the Culebras wanted to send a message, they would have left him in the streets of San José," Rojas said quietly. "Not dragged him up a volcano in the middle of a thunderstorm."

Rojas reached out and pulled back the edge of Delgado’s ruined suit jacket. The mobster’s shirt was torn at the collar, and underneath his fingernails, Rojas saw something that didn't belong on a coffee plantation. It was pale, fine, crushed white sand.

Rojas looked down at Delgado’s expensive leather loafers. They weren't coated in the dark, heavy volcanic soil of the Central Valley. They were stained with a chalky, reddish dust.

"He wasn't killed here," Rojas said, standing up. "He was dumped here to make it look like a syndicate hit."

"You're seeing ghosts again, Teo," Salazar scoffed. "Where else would he have been?"

"That red dust," Rojas pointed. "You only find that specific clay composition in the dry forests up north. And the white sand under his nails..." Rojas paused, his mind already piecing together a map of the country. "He wasn't fighting a rival cartel tonight. He was on a beach in Guanacaste. And someone went through a hell of a lot of trouble to drive his body five hours across the country just to point us in the wrong direction."

Salazar glared at him. "The report says syndicate hit."

"Write whatever you want, Captain," Rojas said, turning his back and walking toward his Land Cruiser. "I'm going to the coast."
